# What do I want?

The agent did exactly what you asked, and it was not what you wanted. The ask was a feeling with a direction, and nobody wrote down where it ends. This skill turns the feeling into one statement and a test before any work starts.

## The principles
1. A want is a feeling with a direction. Understand it before you write a plan.
2. While the want is unclear, do not solve. Show the readings, the tensions, and the one question that changes the picture.
3. Your options are guesses, not the whole space. Say so, and let the person answer in their own words.

## Steps
1. Read the want. Do not start the task and do not change anything yet.
2. In under six lines, show two or three different readings of what they might mean, one line each, with what each reading assumes.
3. Ask the one to three questions whose answers would change the result most (often: who is it for, what changes for them when it works, what must stay as it is). Give two to four options per question, mark your best guess, and end with "or tell me in your own words".
4. If an answer corrects you or adds a new want, go back to step 2. At most three rounds, then write with what you know.
5. Write the statement:
   - **I want:** one sentence in their words: who it is for and what changes for them.
   - **Done when:** one to three checks a stranger could answer yes or no. Use a number when there is one, and "the owner says yes to X" when it is taste.
   - **Not:** what must stay as it is.
   - **Open:** what is still unknown. Never guess it.
6. Ask "Is this what you want?" Start the work only after a yes.

## Rules
- Their words beat yours. Keep their phrases in the statement.
- Never invent a value to finish the statement. A gap goes under Open.
- If the request already has a clear end and a done-test, say so in one line and go.
